# Purpose–Benefit–Experience–Feature Framework

## Model

The framework uses four layers:

1. **Purpose** — the ultimate change or outcome the strategy exists to advance.
2. **Benefits** — the value customers or beneficiaries must receive for that purpose to advance.
3. **Experience** — what customers must be able to do, perceive, or feel to obtain those benefits.
4. **Features** — the product capabilities, services, policies, or operational mechanisms that enable the experience.

Use `PBEF` only as internal shorthand. Do not present it as an established external acronym unless a source does so.

## First resolve whose purpose

“Purpose” can mean different things. State its owner and scope explicitly:

- customer purpose or job;
- product purpose;
- organizational mission;
- initiative outcome;
- societal or ecosystem purpose.

When several purposes matter, represent them separately and show their relationship. Do not conceal tension between customer and business purposes.

## Reasoning directions

### Top-down: strategic design

Use this direction to decide what should exist:

> To advance **[purpose]**, customers need **[benefits]**. Those benefits require **[experience]**, enabled by **[features or capabilities]**.

Top-down questions:

- What must become true if the purpose is achieved?
- Which benefits are necessary rather than merely attractive?
- What experience causes or enables each benefit?
- What is the smallest credible capability set for that experience?

### Bottom-up: execution proof

Use this direction to explain or audit what already exists:

> We provide **[features]** to support **[experience]**, so customers obtain **[benefits]**, contributing to **[purpose]**.

Bottom-up questions:

- What behavior or experience does this feature actually change?
- What customer value follows from that change?
- How does that value contribute to the stated purpose?
- What evidence supports each step?

Bottom-up reasoning must not become post-hoc justification. A coherent sentence is not evidence of causality.

## Layer tests

### Purpose

A useful purpose is directional, consequential, and specific enough to constrain choices. It is not a slogan, feature, activity, or unowned metric.

Test:

- Whose purpose is it?
- What changes if it is achieved?
- Which choices would it rule out?
- At what time horizon and system boundary does it apply?

### Benefits

Benefits describe value received by the customer or beneficiary. They may be functional, economic, emotional, social, or risk-reducing.

Test:

- Who receives the benefit?
- Is it an outcome rather than an activity or output?
- Is it observable or measurable?
- Is the benefit necessary or merely correlated with the purpose?

### Experience

Experience describes the behavior, perception, journey, or operating condition through which value is realized. It is broader than interface design.

Test:

- What must the customer be able to do, understand, trust, or feel?
- At which moments or across which journey does this occur?
- Does the experience plausibly cause the benefit?
- What friction, failure, or tradeoff could prevent it?

### Features

Features include product functionality, service mechanisms, policies, content, human operations, and enabling capabilities.

Test:

- What specific experience does the feature enable or improve?
- Is it necessary, sufficient, complementary, or merely convenient?
- Could a simpler mechanism create the same experience?
- Is the feature being defended because it exists rather than because it is valuable?

## Link tests

Test every important adjacent-layer link:

1. **Specificity:** both ends of the link are concrete enough to evaluate.
2. **Mechanism:** the way one layer enables the next is explained.
3. **Adjacency:** the story does not skip a missing layer.
4. **Scope:** both statements refer to compatible users, contexts, and time horizons.
5. **Counterfactual:** removing the lower element would materially weaken the upper outcome.
6. **Alternatives:** simpler or different mechanisms have been considered.
7. **Evidence:** the link is observed, supported, assumed, or unknown.
8. **Confidence:** confidence reflects evidence quality, not narrative fluency.

## Relationship model

The structure is many-to-many:

- a feature can support several experiences;
- an experience can require several features;
- an experience can generate several benefits;
- a benefit can contribute to several purposes.

Use a matrix or graph when a simple pyramid hides important relationships.

## Failure patterns

- **Orphan feature:** no credible path to an experience and benefit.
- **Experience gap:** a required experience lacks enabling capabilities.
- **Unsupported benefit:** a value claim lacks a plausible experience or evidence.
- **Purpose theatre:** an inspiring purpose does not constrain or connect to execution.
- **Feature laundering:** weak work is made strategic through vague upward language.
- **Metric substitution:** a proxy metric is treated as the purpose or customer benefit.
- **Layer collapse:** purpose, benefits, experience, and features are mixed in one statement.
- **False pyramid:** one-to-one hierarchy is forced onto a many-to-many system.
- **Post-hoc narrative:** bottom-up coherence is mistaken for proof that the original decision was sound.

## Evidence states

Label important claims and links as:

- **Observed:** supported directly by credible data or research.
- **Supported:** multiple signals make the claim plausible.
- **Hypothesis:** a testable belief with limited evidence.
- **Assumption:** currently accepted for planning but not validated.
- **Unknown:** material information is missing.
- **Contested:** credible evidence points in different directions.

For weak or missing evidence, specify the smallest useful validation step.
